# Competitive teardown — scheduling-tool landing pages

Prepared for the marketing lead ahead of the Cadence landing-page brief. Scope: three
rival scheduling products, read live on their own marketing sites (not reviews of them),
on 2026-08-26. All three pages were reachable, so no substitution was needed.

Products covered: **Calendly** (calendly.com), **Cal.com** (cal.com), **SavvyCal**
(savvycal.com).

---

## 1. Calendly

**Headline:** "All the work around meetings, handled."

**Subhead:** "From AI-powered scheduling to automated meeting recaps and follow-ups, get
the busywork done with fewer tools and less effort."

**Primary CTA:** "Sign up with Google" — the first, most visually prominent button in the
hero, directly under the subhead, with "Sign up with Microsoft" and "Sign up with email"
as smaller alternatives beside it. It asks the visitor to create an account before seeing
the product. A second CTA, "Get started for free," sits in the top nav.

**Handling "why not just email":** Never argued directly in prose. It is dramatized as a
workflow step — "A client emails asking to meet. Share a booking link or ask Callie, your
AI assistant, to find times that work for everyone" — and answered again lower down: "Add
Callie to any email thread to coordinate scheduling on your behalf without switching tools
or sacrificing control." The objection is handled by showing the tool living *inside* the
email thread rather than replacing it, not by stating a case against email.

**Steal:** The "add Callie to the thread" framing. It concedes email is where the
conversation actually happens instead of pretending the visitor will abandon it, which
makes the pitch more credible than a flat "stop emailing."

**Avoid:** Three sign-up options stacked in the hero (Google / Microsoft / email) before
the visitor has seen a single booking page or calendar view — the page asks for a
decision about identity before it has shown anything worth deciding on.

## 2. Cal.com

**Headline:** "The better way to schedule your meetings"

**Subhead:** "A fully customizable scheduling software for individuals, businesses taking
calls and developers building scheduling platforms where users meet users."

**Primary CTA:** "Sign up with Google," filled/colored and first in the hero, with
"Sign up with email" as the outlined secondary option beside it. Same pattern as
Calendly: account creation is the ask, immediately, above any product view.

**Handling "why not just email":** Not addressed at all, in any form we could find on the
page or in its FAQ. The closest adjacent claim is about double-booking, not email: "We'll
handle all the cross-referencing, so you don't have to worry about double bookings." The
FAQ ("Frequently asked questions") answers three questions, all about pricing and
industry fit — none about how the tool replaces a scheduling conversation.

**Steal:** "We'll handle all the cross-referencing, so you don't have to worry about
double bookings" — a single concrete failure mode named and closed in one sentence, no
adjective-stacking.

**Avoid:** The FAQ answers read as keyword-stuffed SEO copy rather than answers — e.g. the
pricing answer repeats "scheduling software for small business" / "meeting scheduling
software" / "booking software" across a single paragraph instead of just stating the
tiers. It reads like it was written for a search engine, not for the person asking the
question, which works against trust exactly where trust is being tested.

## 3. SavvyCal

**Headline:** "The fresh way to find a time to meet."

**Subhead:** "You'll love it for the flexible controls to keep your calendar sane. They'll
love it for the ultra-convenient booking experience."

**Primary CTA:** "Try SavvyCal risk-free," directly below the subhead in the hero — the
only button at that level. Unlike Calendly and Cal.com, the ask is framed as a trial
("risk-free"), not an identity/sign-up decision.

**Handling "why not just email":** Handled through a specific interaction, not a
claim about email itself. The "Calendar Overlay" section: "The recipient can view their
calendar events on top of your scheduling link, to save them the hassle of clicking back
and forth." Nearby, on the page describes the older click-a-day-then-click-a-time
interface it replaced. The word "email" itself does not appear in this argument — the
objection being answered is calendar back-and-forth, which is adjacent to but not the
same as the email objection the brief names.

**Steal:** The subhead's two-audience structure — "You'll love it... They'll love it..."
— names both people in a scheduling exchange (the sender and the recipient) in one
sentence, which is the actual objection ("why not email") in miniature: two people, one
of whom is being asked to do the work.

**Avoid:** The primary CTA's promise ("risk-free") answers a pricing objection the visitor
has not raised yet, before the page has shown them what they're trying risk-free — it
answers a question out of order.

---

## Patterns and the open position

**Three patterns, common to all three:**

1. Every headline is a superlative claim about the *process* of scheduling — "all the
   work... handled," "the better way to schedule," "the fresh way to find a time" — not
   about an outcome (fewer no-shows, hours back, revenue). The comparison is tool-to-tool,
   never tool-to-consequence.
2. Every primary hero CTA asks for a commitment — sign up, sign up, try risk-free —
   before the visitor has seen the actual booking page a recipient would experience. The
   product itself is not shown above the fold on any of the three.
3. None of the three states the "why not just email" objection as a sentence and answers
   it as a sentence. Each answers it sideways, through a named feature (Callie in the
   thread; cross-referencing; calendar overlay) that only works as an answer if the
   visitor connects it to email themselves. On Cal.com it is not answered at all, even
   sideways.

**The position none of them has taken:** naming the objection out loud, near the top of
the page, in plain words, and answering it as a claim rather than leaving it to be
inferred from a feature list. All three write for the person who owns the calendar link
(the sender); SavvyCal's subhead is the one exception that speaks to both sides, but even
it never says "email" or "inbox." Cadence can open by stating the trade plainly — say what
emailing back and forth actually costs the two people in the thread, in the same breath as
the headline — instead of trusting a feature name to make the same argument by
implication. That is unclaimed ground on all three pages we read.
